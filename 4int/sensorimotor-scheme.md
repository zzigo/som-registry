# Sensorimotor Scheme

The body's accumulated patterns of coordination with a specific instrument, developed through sustained practice into a sub-cognitive operational layer. Once established, a sensorimotor scheme allows the performer to navigate the instrument's spatial network without conscious route-finding — the fingers "know" the fingerboard; the embouchure adjusts without deliberate thought.

## Theoretical Framework

Tullberg (2022:6), drawing on Di Paolo et al. (2017) and O'Reagan and Noë's Sensorimotor Contingency Theory (SMC), frames sensorimotor schemes as the fundamental unit of analysis for affordances of musical instruments. SMC holds that perception consists of knowledge of sensorimotor contingencies — the structural rules of how sensory input changes with motor action. Playing a musical instrument is the progressive acquisition of these contingencies at increasingly fine resolution.

Malafouris (2013) in Material Engagement Theory describes this as the transactional intertwining of cognition and material: the hand and the instrument co-shape each other. The instrument is not external to cognition but constitutive of it.

Tullberg's concept of **spatial networks** (following DeSousa 2017) extends this: the internalized scheme includes perceptual content dependent on both the physical layout of the instrument and idiomatic musical conventions. These networks "reside in the musician's fingers" (Leman et al. 2017:177) — they are amodal and not reducible to visual memory of the keyboard layout.

## Acquisition and Fragility

The sensorimotor scheme is developed over time through practice and is vulnerable:
- Physical injury can permanently alter it (Tullberg 2022:7: tendinitis, hand problems)
- The instrument itself can change (strings bursting, keys sticking)
- New performance contexts can disrupt established schemes

Crucially, the scheme is also transferable: a pianist learning the flute brings spatial network patterns from the piano keyboard. A musician learning another tuning system can disrupt and rebuild spatial networks (DeSousa 2017: Rosenwinkel's radical retuning).

## Scheme Depth Scale

| Value | Operational meaning |
|---|---|
| 0.0 | No scheme — conscious operation required for all gestures |
| 0.3 | Emerging scheme — familiar gestures automatic, novel situations require attention |
| 0.5 | Partial scheme — core vocabulary internalized, complex passages still deliberate |
| 0.7 | Advanced scheme — most performance operative subconsciously, attention available for expression |
| 1.0 | Full integration — scheme indistinguishable from body schema; virtuosity |

## For New Instruments

For novel instruments with nonlinear or non-idiomatic response characteristics, initial scheme value is ~0.0–0.2. Standard sensorimotor contingencies do not transfer from other instruments — the performer must acquire an entirely new set of sensorimotor rules for how gesture maps to acoustic output. This is a design challenge but also an expressive opportunity: new schemes generate new musical vocabularies.
